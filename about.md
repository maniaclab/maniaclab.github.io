The MANIAC Lab is a group of physicists, engineers and research software engineers in the Enrico Fermi Institute at the University of Chicago. We build and operate the computing that frontier experiments need: the Midwest Tier2 Center and the Analysis Facility for ATLAS, data services for XENON, SPT and SBND, and the platforms, from SLATE and VC3 to RP1, Elwood and AEGIS, where new ideas are tried before they go into production.

Our work runs along two long threads. The first is grid and high-throughput computing: from GriPhyN and iVDGL in 2000, through founding membership in the Open Science Grid, to PATh and today's contributions to the Open Science Pool and Data Federation. The second is ATLAS: continuous support of the UChicago ATLAS group since 2002 and the U.S. ATLAS Operations Program since 2005. Around them grew the platform, software, networking and security projects below, and most recently the agentic systems that we expect to define the next decade.

## The name

Our (admittedly frivolous) name is a nod to the University of Chicago's first computer, MANIAC III. Built in 1961 at the Institute for Computer Research, MANIAC III, short for Mathematical Analyzer, Numerical Integrator, and Computer (Model III), replaced vacuum tubes with solid-state components: 20,000 diodes, 12,000 transistors and 16K words of 48-bit core memory. Its most novel feature was unnormalized significance arithmetic, which let users track how the precision of a result degraded through a computation. It was developed under the leadership of Nicholas Metropolis (SB 1937, PhD 1941), who carried forward and extended the computer designs he had helped build at Los Alamos after World War II.
